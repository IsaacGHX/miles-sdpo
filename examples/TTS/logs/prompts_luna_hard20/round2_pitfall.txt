You are given one or more worked attempts that were graded WRONG, possibly together with a ground-truth answer. Extract 1–3 specific, reusable error-prevention skills. Each block must be based on an error actually visible in an attempt; do not invent an error merely because the final result disagrees with the ground truth.

Output EXACTLY this format and nothing else:

[Error]
<Identify the concrete invalid inference, assumption, omitted case, model mismatch, arithmetic failure, or missing proof obligation visible in the attempt. Do not state the original problem’s answer.>
[Rule]
<State a general, operational prevention rule: tell a future solver exactly what to parse, calculate, enumerate, compare, or prove before accepting the conclusion. Include hypotheses, quantifiers, endpoint conventions, and validation tests when relevant.>
[Example]
<Give a short independent example with different symbols, values, and context. Show the diagnostic check or contrast. Do not solve, echo, or encode the supplied problem.>

Hard constraints:
- Use the literal headers [Error], [Rule], and [Example] for every block.
- Output only the blocks: no introduction, conclusion, commentary, repaired solution, answer, or confidence statement.
- Use only errors evidenced by the supplied attempts. If the exact failed step cannot be localized, describe only the directly supported proof gap or model ambiguity; do not fabricate a cause from the ground truth.
- Do not use the ground truth to reverse-engineer, encode, or hint at the answer, nor to identify an unshown erroneous step.
- Abstract away all source-specific names, symbols, constants, equations, diagrams, and distinctive intermediate values. Examples must be genuinely independent, not a relabeled version of the supplied problem.
- Never state the original final answer, requested quantity, answer choice, count, parameter, or any intermediate value that determines it.
- Make every [Rule] actionable and falsifiable. Replace “be careful” with a prescribed test, exhaustive argument, inequality, case split, or verification.
- Distinguish a wrong conclusion from an insufficient justification. If an attempt gives a candidate without proving it, say that maximality, existence, divisibility, or another required property was not established; do not assert that the candidate itself is false unless the attempt demonstrates that.
- For an ambiguity or apparent inconsistency, first parse the literal domain, quantifiers, fixed data, endpoint conventions, and requested quantity. Flag a missing target or unbounded free variable precisely; do not silently impose an intended restriction. Do not let an ambiguity diagnosis replace a valid solution to a well-posed interpretation.
- For counting and expectation, identify the sample space, weighting, independence, repeated choices, fixed starting conditions, and whether a deterministic strategy is being confused with a random experiment.
- For extremal graph, inequality, optimization, or existence arguments, separate lower-bound constructions from universal upper bounds and prove both directions needed for a threshold or optimum. Cover the full parameter range, not just sampled cases, and check exceptional denominators or degenerate cases.
- For algebra, polynomial composition, and series, track signs, absolute values, orders, unknown coefficients, convergence, and all terms that can affect the claimed coefficient or bound. Do not strengthen a remainder estimate until the omitted terms are shown to cancel or be negligible.
- For arithmetic forms, normalize first and verify that the expression satisfies the stipulated representation before extracting parameters.
- For geometry, verify every claimed symmetry, tangency, perpendicularity, intersection, equal-area, or equal-volume property from the stated hypotheses rather than from a diagram.
- For finite searches, prove that the search range is exhaustive and independently verify every surviving candidate and every maximality claim.
- Prefer a few sharply targeted blocks over many generic warnings. Each example must illustrate the prevention rule, not merely repeat it.
